How Jelly Alert works

Can public ocean and wind data tell us where jellyfish will be a few days ahead? For fishers that means where to avoid; for harvesters, where to find them. This prototype tests it for two species in Danish inner waters.

A study prototype for DTU course 25352 Blue Bioeconomy Innovation, not a product. Built .

What was tested

The model learns from past years and is then tested on years it never saw. The map only shows the test years, because showing years the model learned from would be showing it the answers.

How good it is

The score is AUC: how often the model ranks a real jellyfish sighting above a sighting of other sea life. 0.5 is a coin flip, 1.0 is perfect. The dashed line is a calendar-only guess ("is it jellyfish season?"). The model is only useful where it beats that line.

Where the data comes from

All inputs are public. Nothing is simulated or filled in: days or places without data are left out.

    How this compares with GoJelly

    The EU project GoJelly (2018 to 2021, coordinated by the University of Southern Denmark) built the GoJelly Risk Map. It follows moon jellies through their life cycle, from polyps on the seabed to adult jellyfish, and lets them drift with modelled ocean currents. It explains where blooms could come from. Jelly Alert asks a narrower, practical question: where will jellyfish be in the next few days, and was that right?

    Where GoJelly is stronger. It explains why blooms happen and can test what-if scenarios, such as a polyp bed at a given site (polyps are the small seabed stage that releases young jellyfish). Jelly Alert only finds patterns: it cannot say where jellyfish come from, and it predicts where they are likely, not how many.

    How they fit together. The GoJelly authors name citizen observations as a way to validate their model. Jelly Alert's sighting reports are that channel, and its held-out test is the kind of check their framework still needs.
